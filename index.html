Common types of cybercrime

A working reference for the scams that cause the most damage, grouped by severity, not by how common they are.

CRITICAL — acts fast, hard to reverse

SIM swap

Your number is ported to a new SIM using forged ID, giving the attacker your OTPs and calls.

Red flag: sudden total loss of signal with no explanation.

Ransomware

Files are silently encrypted, often through a malicious attachment or macro, then held for payment.

Red flag: an "enable content" prompt on a file you weren't expecting.

Deepfake voice scams

A cloned voice of a relative or executive requests urgent funds, timed to prevent verification.

Red flag: urgency paired with a request to keep it quiet.

Business email compromise

An invoice thread is quietly hijacked and bank details are swapped mid-conversation.

Red flag: a last-minute change to payment details.
COMMON — high volume, preventable

Phishing messages

A spoofed bank or delivery message pushes you to a fake login page under time pressure.

Red flag: a link that doesn't match the sender's real domain.

UPI / QR fraud

A "collect" request or altered QR code is disguised as a payment you're supposed to receive.

Red flag: entering a PIN to receive money — you never need to.

Job & task scams

A too-good offer requests a small "registration" or "activation" fee upfront.

Red flag: any legitimate job that asks you to pay first.
WATCH — quieter, compounds over time

Credential stuffing

A password reused across sites resurfaces in a breach dump and is tried everywhere else you use it.

Red flag: a login alert from a service you haven't used in months.

Fake tech support

A pop-up or cold call convinces you to install remote-access software "to fix" a problem you don't have.

Red flag: anyone asking to remote into your device unprompted.

How to spot a scam in the moment

Most scams share the same handful of tells, no matter what channel they arrive through. Learn these and you'll catch the majority of attempts before they go further.

Manufactured urgency

"Act now or lose access" is a pressure tactic, not a real deadline. Genuine institutions rarely demand instant action.

Unusual payment requests

Being asked to pay via gift cards, crypto, or a "collect" UPI request is a near-certain sign of fraud.

Mismatched sender details

Check the actual email address or link domain, not just the display name — spoofing the name is trivial.

Requests to keep it secret

Being told not to tell family, colleagues, or your bank is designed to stop you from getting a second opinion.

Too good to be true

Unusually high returns, easy money, or a job that pays well for almost no work is bait, not opportunity.

Unsolicited remote access

No legitimate support team will ask to remotely control your device out of nowhere.

Before you click, tap, or pay — ask

Did I initiate this?
If you didn't start the interaction, treat any request for money or information with suspicion.

Can I verify independently?
Call the organization back using a number you look up yourself, not one given in the message.

Would they really ask this way?
Banks and government bodies don't request OTPs, PINs, or passwords over call or chat.

What happens if I wait?
If pausing to check would "ruin" the deal, the deal was never real.

What to do if it happens to you

Acting quickly matters, the faster a fraudulent transaction is reported, the better the chance of getting funds frozen before they move further.

01

Stop and disconnect

End the call, close the page, or disconnect the device from the internet if malware or remote access is involved. Don't make any further payments while things are unclear.

02

Preserve the evidence

Screenshot the messages, note the phone number or account involved, and save transaction IDs or reference numbers. Don't delete anything, even if it's unsettling to look at.

03

Report it immediately

Call the national cybercrime helpline or file a report on the portal below. For financial fraud, also alert your bank's fraud line directly so they can flag the transaction.

04

Secure what's left

Change passwords on any related accounts, enable two-factor authentication, and keep an eye on statements for follow-up attempts — a first scam is often followed by others.
